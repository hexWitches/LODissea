The Journey Begins

Europeana is a shared digital space where millions of cultural artifacts - paintings, photographs, manuscripts, films and sounds - from thousands of institutions across Europe converge into a vast and open sea. It sits at the heart of the EU's common data space for cultural heritage, anchored by three principles: heritage that's easy to find and reuse, a network built on shared contribution rather than a single institution, and data cared for with the same precision as the objects it describes.

These are the values Europeana states about itself, but do they hold beneath the surface? Is contribution genuinely mutual and spread across many institutions, or concentrated between few providers? Is the underlying data as reliable as claimed, consistently and equally across countries?

There is only one way to answer those questions: dive deep and see whether Europeana's promise holds against its own data.

The Portugal Bias

Portugal's initial partecipation rate (7.22%) is artificially inflated because crowdsourced Wikidata only records 554 physical institutions, far below the 3,395 verified in the official INE administrative census.

When we override the denominator using the official census baseline, the rate drops to 1.18%. Clicking the button below visually shifts Portugal's bubble to the left, revealing the true macroeconomic correlation.

Failed routing

Once the Portugal census override is applied, a clear linear trajectory emerges between public spending and institutional mobilization: nations that invest more of their GDP in culture generally bring a larger proportion of their physical GLAMs into the Europeana ecosystem. However, evaluating bubble size (artifact volume) exposes a striking divergence. France is a primary outlier, leading all observed countries in cultural investment (1.40% of GDP), yet its mobilization rate remains suppressed at 1.89%, contributing a modest 4.7M items.


France's contribution reveals a single ship doing much of the work: the Bibliothèque nationale de France.
But is France an exception, or does Europeana's sea rely on a handful of large vessels carrying entire national fleets? To answer this, we turn from countries to providers and examine who is really steering Europe's digital heritage.

Who sends knowledge to the Europeana sea?

Behind the vast archive of Europeana stand 1002 providers from the six selected countries, each loading their cultural treasures onto the digital vessel. But who sails Europeana's waters, and how heavily loaded is each provider?

High Tide, Low Tide

  • In most countries a single provider dominates, contributing the majority of all national items.
  • The Netherlands isn't just home to the highest number of providers; it also hosts the one provider carrying the heaviest cultural heritage load of all.
  • Spain and Italy show the most distributed contribution, with several institutions sharing the load.

Where Digitization Happens

Concentration leaves its mark on the map as well. Mapping the top 20 providers in each country reveals distinct geographical patterns.

  • France once again stands out as the most concentrated case, with its contribution clustered around a small number of locations.
  • Portugal and Spain display a much broader geographical spread, with providers extending beyond the mainland to the Azores, Madeira, and the Canary Islands.
  • Italy's network is also distributed across the peninsula, but none of its largest contributors are located on the islands.

On the other side of the ocean:

Two of the top 20 providers — Internet Archive and IMSLP/Petrucci Music Library — are based outside Europe. These appear to act as digital aggregators rather than individual cultural institutions, collecting content from multiple sources before contributing it to Europeana.

What kinds of knowledge sail these waters?

Not every vessel sails with the same inventory. We look at how items are distributed across institution categories - libraries, archives, museums, natural history collections, audiovisual archives, and more - comparing composition to reveal each nation's cultural identity through its data.

  • Libraries & archives dominate globally, except in the Netherlands and Portugal where natural history collections lead instead.
  • Italy stands out for its audiovisual richness, with audiovisual/film archives making up 31.7%.

Category Composition by Country

Why these categories?

Each provider is classified into an institution-type category derived from the data itself, rather than defined upfront. Classification draws primarily on the provider's Wikidata entity - its description, name, and structured properties - enriching Europeana's own records with an external, linked open data source.

Exploring Europeana's data reveals a clear limitation: categories aren't perfectly aligned across providers. This difficulty led us toward a more fundamental question: the quality and completeness of Europeana's metadata itself.

The Project

LODissea is an exploratory research, investigating the open and collaborative environment of Europeana. Specifically, we examine the scale and distribution of data providers, analyzing who they are and what institutional categories they represent, and the overall structural quality of their data. The selected countries for this study are: Italy, Germany, Spain, Portugal, France, the Netherlands. Data retrieved from Europeana are enriched using Wikidata and Eurostat databases.

The project has been carried out for the final exam of Information Visualization, within the Digital Humanities and Digital Knowledge course at the University of Bologna.

All additional material produced for the project can be found in the Github Repository.
